A musty smell — stale, earthy, a bit like damp cardboard — is almost always caused by **moisture**. Where there’s ongoing damp, mould and mildew grow and release the compounds you can smell, often before you can see anything.

So the fix isn’t an air freshener. It’s finding where the moisture comes from, drying the room out, and cleaning away any growth. Here’s how.

## Common causes of a musty smell in a room

### Poor ventilation

Rooms that are rarely aired — spare bedrooms, basements, rooms with windows kept shut — trap moisture from breathing, showers and cooking. Without airflow, humidity stays high and surfaces never fully dry.

### Condensation

Water droplets on windows in the morning, damp window frames, or black spots in corners and behind furniture are signs of condensation. Warm, moist air hits cold surfaces and turns back into water, which feeds mould. It’s especially common in bedrooms in cold weather.

### Drying laundry indoors

A single load of washing drying on a rack releases litres of water into the air. Doing this in a closed room is one of the quickest ways to make it smell damp.

### Hidden leaks

A slow leak from a roof, gutter, window seal, radiator valve or pipe can soak into walls, ceilings or under flooring out of sight. Look for stains, peeling paint, bubbling plaster, warped skirting boards or a floor that feels soft.

### Carpets, soft furnishings and stored items

Carpets, mattresses, sofas, curtains and boxes of stored clothes absorb moisture and odours. Items stored against cold external walls are particularly prone to mildew.

### Air conditioning units

Window and split AC units collect condensation. If the drip tray or drain is blocked, or the coils are dirty, water sits inside the unit and a musty smell blows out when it runs.

### Basements and ground floors

Below-ground rooms are cooler and can draw in moisture through walls and floors, so they are naturally more humid.

## How to find the source

1. **Follow your nose.** The smell is usually strongest near the source — sniff inside wardrobes, behind furniture, under sinks and near windows.
2. **Check humidity.** A digital hygrometer costs little and tells you whether the room’s air is the problem. Readings consistently above 60% mean the room is too damp.
3. **Look for visible signs:** black, green or white spots; water stains; condensation; peeling wallpaper.
4. **Move furniture away from walls** and look behind it, especially on external walls.
5. **Check the obvious water sources:** under sinks, around the bath or shower, radiators, the washing machine, and the AC unit’s drip tray.

## How to get rid of a musty smell

### 1. Fix any water problem first

Repair leaks, clear gutters, reseal windows and fix plumbing drips. Nothing else will last until the water stops.

### 2. Dry the room out

- Open windows for at least 10–15 minutes a day, ideally on opposite sides of the room or home to create a cross-draught.
- Run a **dehumidifier** until humidity drops below 60%. Empty the tank regularly.
- Use extractor fans when cooking and showering, and keep them running for a while afterwards.
- Leave a small gap between furniture and external walls so air can circulate.

### 3. Clean small areas of mould or mildew

For small patches on hard surfaces, clean with water and detergent, scrub, and dry the surface thoroughly. Wear rubber gloves, eye protection and a mask while you work, and keep the window open.

> [!WARNING]
> Never mix bleach with ammonia or other cleaning products — the combination produces toxic fumes. If mould covers a large area (the US EPA uses roughly 10 square feet, about 1 m², as a guide), keeps coming back, or is caused by sewage or contaminated water, get professional help.

### 4. Deal with fabrics and soft furnishings

Wash curtains, bedding, cushion covers and clothes on the hottest cycle their care labels allow, and dry them fully — outdoors if possible. For carpets and mattresses, sprinkle bicarbonate of soda (baking soda), leave it for several hours, then vacuum thoroughly. Heavily mildewed carpet or underlay may need replacing.

### 5. Absorb what’s left

Once the source is fixed, bowls of bicarbonate of soda or bags of activated charcoal help absorb lingering odours. They support the fix; they don’t replace it.

## Keeping the smell from coming back

- Keep humidity between about 30% and 50% and check it with a hygrometer in problem rooms.
- Dry laundry outdoors or in a vented tumble dryer; if you must dry indoors, do it in a small room with the door shut, the window open and a dehumidifier running.
- Air rooms every day, even in winter — a short, wide-open window is better than a window left on the latch all day.
- Keep wardrobes and cupboards a little away from external walls, and don’t overpack them.
- Clean AC drip trays and filters as the manufacturer recommends.

If the smell is more like a sewer than damp, the cause is usually a drain or toilet instead. See [what to do when your house smells like sewage](/why-does-my-house-smell-like-sewage).

Damp homes often feel dusty too, because humidity helps dust mites thrive. If that sounds familiar, see our guide to [why your home gets dusty and how to reduce it](/why-is-my-house-so-dusty).

## When to call a professional

Get a qualified damp specialist, surveyor or plumber if you find a hidden leak, extensive mould, damp patches that keep spreading, or rot in wood. If anyone in the home has worsening breathing problems, speak to a doctor. Renters should report damp and mould to their landlord in writing as soon as they notice it.
