To find air leaks in your house, **walk around on a cold or windy day and feel for drafts with a damp hand, or hold a lit incense stick near windows, doors, outlets, and pipes and watch for smoke that moves sideways.** The biggest leaks are often hidden in the attic and basement, so check there too. Most leaks can be sealed in an afternoon with caulk, weatherstripping, and foam.

The Department of Energy says air leaks can account for **up to 30 percent** of a home's heating and cooling energy use. Finding and sealing them makes rooms more comfortable and lowers your bills.

## 7 easy ways to find air leaks

### 1. The damp hand test

Wet your hand and slowly move it around window frames, door edges, outlets, and baseboards. Moving air feels cool on wet skin, so drafts are easy to notice. This works best on a cold or windy day.

### 2. The incense or candle test

Close all windows and doors, turn off the furnace and fans, and turn on bathroom and kitchen exhaust fans. This pulls air out of the house so outside air comes in through leaks. Then slowly move a lit incense stick around likely spots. If the smoke blows sideways or gets sucked in, you found a leak.

**Safety:** Keep flames away from curtains and anything that can burn, and never leave them unattended.

### 3. The dollar bill test

Close a door or window on a dollar bill. If you can pull it out without any drag, the seal is too loose and air is getting through.

### 4. The flashlight test

At night, have one person shine a bright flashlight around a door or window frame from inside while another person watches from outside. Light shining through a gap means air can get through too.

### 5. Look for clues

- **Dirty spots** on carpet edges or insulation, where air has been filtering through
- **Spider webs** that move in corners
- **Light** coming in under doors
- **Rooms that feel much colder** than the rest of the house

### 6. Use a thermal camera

A thermal camera or a phone add-on shows cold spots as dark colors. Some hardware stores and libraries lend them out.

### 7. Get a blower door test

For the most complete check, hire an energy auditor. They use a big fan in a doorway to pull air out of the house, which makes every leak easy to find. Some utility companies offer free or low-cost energy audits.

## The most common air leak spots

| Where | What to check |
|---|---|
| Attic | Attic hatch, gaps around pipes and wires, recessed lights, chimney edges |
| Basement | Where the wood frame sits on the foundation, pipe and wire holes, dryer vent |
| Windows | Gaps around the frame, worn weatherstripping, cracked glazing |
| Doors | Gaps at the bottom, worn weatherstripping, mail slots |
| Walls | Outlets and switches on outside walls, cable and phone holes |
| Outside | Outdoor faucets, where siding meets the foundation, AC lines |
| Other | Window AC units, fireplace dampers, bathroom and kitchen fans |

Many people only check windows and doors. But the biggest leaks are often in the attic and basement, because warm air rises and escapes through the top of the house while cold air is pulled in at the bottom.

## How to seal air leaks

| Leak | Best fix | About how much |
|---|---|---|
| Small gaps under 1/4 inch | Caulk | $5 to $10 a tube |
| Bigger gaps and holes | Expanding spray foam | $5 to $10 a can |
| Moving parts of doors and windows | Weatherstripping | $5 to $20 a roll |
| Gaps under doors | Door sweep | $10 to $25 |
| Outlets on outside walls | Foam outlet gaskets | A few dollars for a pack |
| Attic hatch | Weatherstripping and a foam cover | $20 to $50 |
| Old windows | Window insulation film | $10 to $25 per window |

### Tips for sealing

- **Clean and dry the surface** before using caulk or tape.
- **Use the right caulk.** Silicone works well outside, and paintable acrylic latex is good inside.
- **Turn off power** before putting gaskets behind outlet covers.
- **Do not seal around furnace flues or chimneys with regular foam.** Use fire-rated materials made for high heat.
- **Keep the house breathing.** A very tight home still needs fresh air. Run bathroom and kitchen fans, and keep an eye on humidity.

## Sealing leaks helps with more than energy bills

- **Fewer bugs.** The same gaps let in pests. Our guide on [keeping stink bugs out](/how-to-get-rid-of-stink-bugs) explains how sealing helps.
- **Less condensation.** Cold drafts near windows can lead to wet glass. See [why windows get condensation](/why-is-there-condensation-on-my-windows).
- **Warmer rooms.** If some rooms stay cold even after sealing, your heating system may need attention. Check our guide on [a furnace that blows cold air](/why-is-my-furnace-blowing-cold-air).
- **Less dust** blowing in from the attic and outside.

## When to call a pro

- You cannot find the source of a cold draft
- Your attic has little or no insulation
- You want to seal around chimneys or furnace flues
- You want a full energy audit with a blower door test

## The bottom line

Finding air leaks is easy with a damp hand, an incense stick, or the dollar bill test. Check windows and doors, but do not skip the attic and basement, where the biggest leaks often hide. Most leaks can be sealed with caulk, foam, weatherstripping, and door sweeps for less than $100, and the savings on your heating and cooling bills start right away.
