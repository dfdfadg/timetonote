When the Wi-Fi stops working, the fastest way to fix it is to work out **where** the problem is: your device, your router, or your internet connection itself. One quick question narrows it down.

## First: is it one device or everything?

Check another phone, laptop or tablet on the same Wi-Fi network.

- **Only one device has a problem** → the issue is almost certainly on that device. Go to [fixes for a single device](#fixes-when-only-one-device-cant-connect).
- **Every device has a problem** → the issue is the router, modem or your internet provider. Start with [fixes for the whole network](#fixes-when-no-device-can-get-online).

Also note *how* it’s failing: can’t see the network at all, can’t connect, or connected but pages won’t load (“no internet”). Each points to a different cause.

## Fixes when no device can get online

### 1. Check for an outage

Look at your provider’s service status page or app using mobile data, or check whether neighbours on the same provider are affected. If there’s an outage, you’ll just have to wait.

### 2. Restart the modem and router properly

1. Unplug the power from the modem and the router (if they’re separate boxes).
2. Wait about 30 seconds.
3. Plug the modem in first and wait until its lights are stable (usually a couple of minutes).
4. Then plug in the router and wait for it to fully start.

This clears temporary faults and gets a fresh connection from your provider. It fixes a surprising number of problems.

### 3. Check the lights and cables

Most routers show a status light for the internet connection. Red, orange or flashing lights usually mean the router can’t reach your provider. Check that every cable is firmly connected — especially the one from the wall socket or fibre box — and that nothing has been knocked loose.

### 4. Connect with a cable to test

If you can, plug a laptop directly into the router with an Ethernet cable. If the wired connection works but Wi-Fi doesn’t, the problem is the router’s wireless settings or signal. If neither works, it’s the internet connection — contact your provider.

### 5. Update or reset the router

Log in to your router’s app or admin page and install any firmware update. As a last resort, a factory reset (usually by holding a recessed reset button for 10–30 seconds) returns it to its original settings — but you’ll need to set up the network name and password again, and some providers’ settings. Check your provider’s instructions first.

## Fixes when only one device can’t connect

### 1. Toggle Wi-Fi off and on

Turn Wi-Fi off, wait ten seconds, and turn it on again. Or switch Airplane Mode on and off. This resets the wireless connection.

### 2. Restart the device

A full restart clears network glitches that toggling Wi-Fi doesn’t.

### 3. Forget the network and reconnect

In your device’s Wi-Fi settings, choose the network and select **Forget** (or **Forget This Network**). Then reconnect and enter the password again carefully — passwords are case-sensitive. This fixes problems caused by changed passwords or corrupted saved settings.

### 4. Check the date, time and VPN

An incorrect date or time can stop secure websites from loading. A VPN, security app or custom DNS setting can also block traffic. Temporarily turn off any VPN and see whether the connection returns.

### 5. Update the device

Install any pending system updates — they often include Wi-Fi fixes. If your phone also has trouble charging while you’re at it, our guide on [what to do when an iPhone won’t charge](/iphone-not-charging) covers that step by step.

### 6. Reset network settings

If nothing else works, resetting network settings erases saved Wi-Fi networks, passwords and VPN settings and restores defaults.

- **iPhone:** Settings > General > Transfer or Reset iPhone > Reset > Reset Network Settings.
- **Android:** Settings > System > Reset options > Reset Wi-Fi, mobile & Bluetooth (wording varies by manufacturer).
- **Windows:** Settings > Network & internet > Advanced network settings > Network reset.

## When the Wi-Fi works but is slow or keeps dropping

- **Move closer to the router** or remove obstacles. Walls, floors, mirrors, fish tanks and large appliances weaken signals.
- **Put the router in a central, raised, open spot** — not in a cupboard, behind the TV or on the floor.
- **Try the other band.** 5 GHz is faster up close; 2.4 GHz reaches further.
- **Reduce interference.** Cordless phones, baby monitors and microwaves can disrupt 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi.
- **Check what’s using the connection.** Large downloads, cloud backups and several video streams at once can saturate a slower plan.
- **Consider a mesh system** or a wired access point if some rooms are always weak. Mesh systems usually perform better than basic range extenders.

If you are connected but pages still crawl, our guide to [speeding up a slow internet connection](/why-is-my-internet-so-slow) covers speed tests, router placement and more.

## Quick reference

| What you see | Likely cause | Try this |
| --- | --- | --- |
| No device can get online | Provider outage, modem or router | Check status page; restart modem then router |
| “Connected, no internet” | Router can’t reach provider | Restart; check cables and router lights |
| One device can’t connect | Saved settings or device glitch | Forget network and rejoin; restart device |
| Network not visible | Router off, or wrong band | Check router power; move closer; check 2.4/5 GHz |
| Slow or dropping | Distance, interference, congestion | Move router; switch band; consider mesh |

## When to contact your provider

Call your internet provider if the router’s internet light stays red after a restart, a wired connection doesn’t work either, speeds are consistently far below your plan, or the router provided by them seems faulty. They can test the line from their end and replace equipment if needed.
