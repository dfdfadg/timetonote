To install a water filtration system, **pick the right type for your home, turn off the water, connect the filter to your cold water line, then flush it and check for leaks.** Most under-sink filters take less than 2 hours and only need basic tools. A whole-house system takes more work because it goes on your main water line, so many people call a plumber for that one.

Before you buy anything, find out what is actually in your water. The right filter depends on the problem you want to fix. A filter that removes a chlorine taste will not always remove lead, and a filter made for lead will not fix low pressure or hard water.

This guide walks you through each step in plain language, from choosing a filter to your first glass of clean water.

## Step 1: Find out what is in your water

You cannot pick the right filter until you know what you are filtering out. Here is how to check.

- **City water:** Your water utility must send out a water quality report every year. It is called a Consumer Confidence Report. You can often find it on your utility's website or on your water bill. It lists what was found in your water and how much.
- **Well water:** No one tests a private well for you. The CDC suggests testing a well at least once a year. Your local health department can often point you to a certified lab.
- **Your own senses:** A bleach or pool smell usually means chlorine. There is a full guide on [why tap water tastes like chlorine](/why-does-my-water-taste-like-chlorine) if that is your main issue. A rotten egg smell points to sulfur, which we cover in [fixing water that smells like rotten eggs](/why-does-my-water-smell-like-rotten-eggs).

Write down the one or two problems you most want to fix. That list will guide every choice you make next.

## Step 2: Choose the right type of filter

There are five common types of home water filters. Here is a simple side-by-side look.

| Filter type | Where it goes | What it filters | DIY level |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pitcher | In your fridge | Drinking water only | No install |
| Faucet-mounted | On the end of your kitchen faucet | One faucet | Very easy |
| Under-sink | Inside the kitchen cabinet | One faucet | Easy to medium |
| Reverse osmosis (RO) | Under the sink, with a small tank | One faucet, very fine filtering | Medium |
| Whole-house | On the main water line | Every tap in the home | Hard |

**Pitcher and faucet filters** are the cheapest and quickest. They are great for renters or if you just want better-tasting drinking water.

**Under-sink filters** give you a steady flow of filtered water without taking up counter space. This is the most popular DIY choice.

**Reverse osmosis systems** push water through a very fine layer that removes many more things, like some dissolved minerals and metals. They waste some water while they work and need a drain connection.

**Whole-house systems** filter water as it enters your home, so your shower, laundry, and every sink get filtered water. They are best for problems like sediment, rust, or chlorine all over the house.

### Look for a real certification

Many filters claim to "remove 99% of contaminants." That claim alone means very little. Look for an **NSF/ANSI certification** on the box, and check that it lists the exact thing you want removed. Here are the common ones:

- **NSF/ANSI 42:** taste and smell, such as chlorine
- **NSF/ANSI 53:** health-related contaminants, such as lead
- **NSF/ANSI 58:** reverse osmosis systems
- **NSF/ANSI 401:** some newer contaminants, like certain medicines

The CDC points out that "meets NSF standards" or "independently tested" is not the same as certified. You can look up any model in NSF's online listings to be sure.

## Step 3: Gather your tools and parts

Most filters come with the parts you need. Read the manual first, because each brand is a little different. For a typical under-sink install, you will need:

- An adjustable wrench (two is even better)
- A drill, if you are mounting the filter on the cabinet wall
- Plumber's tape (also called Teflon tape)
- A bucket and a few old towels
- A flashlight
- A utility knife or tube cutter for plastic tubing
- A tape measure

If your kit includes a separate faucet for filtered water, you may also need a drill bit to make a hole in your sink or countertop. Many sinks already have a spare hole covered with a small cap. Check for one before you drill.

## Step 4: Install an under-sink water filter

This is the most common DIY install. The steps below match most basic under-sink kits.

1. **Clear out the cabinet.** Take everything out from under the sink so you have room to work. Put down a towel and set the bucket under the pipes.
2. **Turn off the cold water.** Find the small shutoff valve on the cold water pipe under the sink and turn it clockwise until it stops. Then open the kitchen faucet to let out the leftover water and pressure.
3. **Pick a spot for the filter.** Hold the filter housing against the cabinet wall. Leave a few inches of space below it, since you will need room to twist off the filter when it is time to change it.
4. **Mount the bracket.** Screw the mounting bracket to the cabinet wall, then hang the filter housing on it.
5. **Connect to the cold water line.** Disconnect the cold water supply line from the shutoff valve. Screw on the adapter or tee fitting that came with your kit. Wrap threads with plumber's tape if the manual says to. Then reattach the faucet supply line to the adapter.
6. **Run the tubing.** Cut the tubing to length with a clean, straight cut. Push one end into the adapter and the other end into the filter's "in" port. Connect a second piece from the filter's "out" port to your filtered water faucet or to the faucet line, based on your kit.
7. **Install the filter faucet (if your kit has one).** Put it in the spare sink hole or a new hole you drilled. Tighten the nut from under the sink.
8. **Turn the water back on slowly.** Open the shutoff valve a little at a time. Watch every connection for drips for a few minutes.
9. **Flush the new filter.** Run water through the filter for the time the manual says, often around 5 minutes. The first water may look cloudy or have black bits of carbon. That is normal and should clear up.

If you see a drip, turn the water off and tighten that fitting about a quarter turn. Do not over-tighten plastic parts, because they can crack.

## Step 5: Install a reverse osmosis system

An RO system installs in almost the same way as an under-sink filter, with a few extra parts.

- **A storage tank:** RO makes water slowly, so a small tank stores it. Set the tank in the cabinet and connect it with the tubing provided.
- **A drain connection:** RO systems flush out waste water. Your kit will include a "drain saddle" that clamps onto your sink's drain pipe. You drill a small hole in the drain pipe through the saddle.
- **A dedicated faucet:** RO water almost always comes from its own small faucet.

Follow the color-coded tubing in your manual closely. After you finish, most makers tell you to **fill and empty the tank once or twice** before you drink the water. This can take a few hours.

## Step 6: Install a whole-house water filter

A whole-house system is a bigger job. If you are not comfortable cutting pipes, this is a good time to call a licensed plumber. Some towns also require a permit, so check with your local building office first.

Here is what the job looks like:

1. **Pick the location.** The filter goes on the main cold water line, after the main shutoff valve and water meter, and before the line goes to your water heater. This is often in a basement, garage, or utility closet.
2. **Shut off the main water.** Then open a faucet on the lowest floor to drain the lines.
3. **Cut the pipe.** Use a pipe cutter to remove a section of pipe where the filter will go.
4. **Add shutoff valves and a bypass.** Many plumbers add a valve before and after the filter, plus a bypass loop. This lets you change filters or skip the system without shutting off water to the whole house.
5. **Connect the filter housing.** Attach the fittings for your pipe type, like copper, PEX, or PVC. Make sure the water flows in the direction of the arrow on the housing.
6. **Keep your electrical ground.** In some homes, the metal water pipe is part of the electrical grounding system. If you cut a metal pipe and add a plastic filter, a **grounding jumper wire** should connect the pipe on both sides. Ask an electrician or plumber if you are not sure.
7. **Turn the water on slowly and check for leaks.** Then run a few taps to push out air and flush the new filter.

It is normal to notice a small drop in water pressure after a whole-house filter goes in. If the drop is big, you may need a larger system. Our guide on [how to boost weak water pressure at home](/how-to-increase-water-pressure-in-house) explains other things to check.

## How much does a water filtration system cost?

Prices depend on the brand and what the system removes. Here are rough ranges to plan with.

| System | Typical unit price | Install cost |
|---|---|---|
| Pitcher | $20 to $50 | None |
| Faucet-mounted | $20 to $60 | None |
| Under-sink | $50 to $300 | Usually DIY |
| Reverse osmosis | $150 to $600 | DIY, or $100 to $300 for a plumber |
| Whole-house | $300 to $3,000 or more | Often $300 to $1,000 or more for a plumber |

Do not forget the cost of **replacement filters**. A cheap system with expensive filters can cost more over a few years than a pricier system with cheap filters. Check filter prices before you buy.

## Keep your filter working

A filter only works if you take care of it. The CDC warns that old filters can stop working and may even let germs grow. Follow these simple habits.

- **Change filters on time.** Pitcher filters often last about 2 months. Under-sink filters usually last 6 to 12 months. Whole-house sediment filters may need changing every 3 to 6 months. Your manual has the exact schedule.
- **Set a reminder.** Put the next change date on your phone calendar the day you install a new filter.
- **Write the date on the filter.** A small piece of tape with the date makes it easy to check later.
- **Flush after a long trip.** If you have been away for a week or more, run the filtered water for a few minutes before drinking it.
- **Watch for warning signs.** Slower flow, a bad taste coming back, or cloudy water all mean it is time for a new filter.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Buying before testing.** You may pay for a filter that does not remove your actual problem.
- **Ignoring certifications.** Always match the NSF standard to the contaminant you care about.
- **Installing on the hot water line.** Most point-of-use filters are made for cold water only. Hot water can damage them.
- **Over-tightening plastic fittings.** Snug is enough. Too tight can crack the part and cause a leak later.
- **Forgetting the flush.** Skipping the first flush can leave carbon dust in your water.
- **Placing the filter where you cannot reach it.** You will need to get to it every few months.

## When to call a plumber

DIY is fine for most small filters. Call a pro if:

- You are installing a whole-house system and have never cut a pipe
- Your pipes are old, rusty, or you are not sure what they are made of
- You need to drill through stone or quartz countertops
- Your town requires a permit
- You still see leaks after tightening the fittings

A good plumber can often finish a whole-house install in a few hours and will make sure it meets local code.

## The bottom line

Installing a water filtration system is easier than most people think. Start by learning what is in your water, then pick a certified filter that removes those exact things. An under-sink or faucet filter is a great weekend project, while a whole-house system is often best left to a plumber. Once it is in, change the filters on time, and you will have cleaner, better-tasting water every day.
