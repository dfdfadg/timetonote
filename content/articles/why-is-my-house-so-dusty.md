A little dust is normal in every home. But if you can write your name on a shelf a few days after cleaning, something is adding dust faster than you’re removing it. The good news: the causes are usually easy to identify, and most of the fixes are cheap.

This guide explains where household dust comes from, the common reasons it builds up quickly, and what to change — starting with the fixes that make the biggest difference.

## What household dust is actually made of

Household dust is a mixture, and the mix varies from home to home. Typical ingredients include:

- **Outdoor particles** — soil, pollen, and traffic and construction dust carried in on shoes or through doors, windows and ventilation.
- **Fibres** from carpets, rugs, bedding, towels, clothing and upholstery.
- **Pet dander and hair**, plus human skin flakes and hair.
- **Cooking and combustion residue** from stoves, candles and fireplaces.
- **Dust mite and insect debris**, especially in humid homes.

Knowing this helps, because it shows dust has two routes in: it’s brought inside, or it’s generated inside. Fixing a dusty house means working on both.

## The most common reasons your house is so dusty

### 1. Your HVAC filter is clogged or too basic

If you have central heating or air conditioning with a forced-air system, all the air in your home passes through its filter many times a day. A clogged filter stops trapping particles, and a very basic one catches only large debris. Check it now — if it’s grey and matted, replace it. Most filters need changing every one to three months, more often with pets.

If your system allows it, a higher-efficiency pleated filter (often rated MERV 11–13) captures much finer dust. Check your system’s manual first: some older systems can’t push enough air through a dense filter.

### 2. Dust is getting in from outside

Gaps around windows, doors, loft hatches and pipe openings let outdoor dust in, especially if you live near a busy road, farmland or construction. Hold your hand near closed windows and doors on a windy day — if you feel a draught, dust can get through too. Weatherstripping and a door sweep are inexpensive and help with heating bills as well.

### 3. Shoes are worn indoors

Shoes carry soil, grit and pollen from every surface you walked on. A good doormat outside, a second mat inside, and taking shoes off at the door noticeably cut the amount of dust that reaches your floors and carpets.

### 4. Pets

Dogs and cats shed hair and dander constantly, and they bring in dirt on their paws and coats. Regular brushing (outdoors if possible), washing pet bedding weekly and vacuuming pet areas more often all help.

### 5. Carpets, rugs and soft furnishings

Carpets and fabric both shed fibres and act as dust reservoirs: every footstep puffs trapped particles back into the air. Thick rugs, heavy curtains, cushions and piles of throws add up. You don’t need to rip up carpets — but they do need vacuuming more frequently than hard floors.

### 6. The way you clean is spreading dust around

This is the most overlooked cause. A dry duster or feather duster mostly relocates dust into the air, where it settles again a few hours later. Vacuums without a sealed system or HEPA filter can blow fine particles out of the exhaust. And cleaning floors *before* dusting high surfaces means dust lands on floors you’ve just cleaned.

If this sounds familiar, our step-by-step guide on [how to clean a dusty house properly](/how-to-clean-a-dusty-house) walks through the right order and tools.

### 7. The air is too dry — or too humid

Very dry air makes static build up, so dust clings to screens, plastics and fabrics. Very humid air encourages dust mites and mould. The US Environmental Protection Agency recommends keeping indoor humidity between roughly 30% and 50%. A cheap hygrometer will tell you where you are.

If your home feels damp as well as dusty, read about [what causes a musty smell in a room](/why-does-my-room-smell-musty) — the same moisture problems are often behind both.

### 8. Bedding and mattresses

You spend hours a day in bed, shedding skin and hair into sheets and pillows. Dust mites feed on it. Washing bedding weekly in hot water, and using zippered covers on mattresses and pillows, reduces a big source of bedroom dust.

### 9. Renovation, clutter and other hidden sources

Recent building work can leave fine dust in ducts, cavities and carpets for weeks. Clutter — stacks of paper, open shelving, ornaments — gives dust more places to settle and makes cleaning slower, so it gets done less often. A tumble dryer with a leaking or poorly connected vent can also blow lint into the house.

## How to reduce dust in your house: what works best

Start at the top of this list. The first few changes usually make the most difference for the least effort.

1. **Replace your HVAC filter** and set a reminder to check it monthly.
2. **Switch to damp microfibre cloths** for dusting — they trap dust instead of spreading it.
3. **Use a vacuum with a HEPA filter**, empty it before it’s full, and vacuum carpets and rugs at least twice a week in busy rooms.
4. **Stop dust at the door** with two mats and a no-shoes habit.
5. **Seal obvious gaps** around windows and doors.
6. **Wash bedding weekly** and wash or shake out throws and cushion covers regularly.
7. **Keep humidity around 30–50%** with a humidifier or dehumidifier as needed.
8. **Consider a HEPA air purifier** for bedrooms or the main living area. Choose one rated for your room size (check its CADR rating).
9. **Declutter surfaces** so they’re quick to wipe.

> [!TIP]
> Dust settles for several hours after you clean. If you dust in the morning, run an air purifier or leave a window open for a while, then do a quick final wipe later in the day.

## Should you get your air ducts cleaned?

Probably not as a routine job. The EPA notes that duct cleaning has not been shown to prevent health problems, and that ducts don’t need cleaning on a schedule. It may be worth it if there is visible mould inside the ducts, signs of rodents or insects, or ducts clogged with debris that is actually blowing out of the vents. Changing filters regularly is usually far more effective.

## When dust might be a sign of something else

Most dusty homes simply need the changes above. Get a professional to take a look if:

- dust appears suddenly in large amounts, or is gritty and unusual in colour;
- you notice black dust around vents or on walls near heaters (this can come from combustion appliances);
- the dust comes with a persistent musty or damp smell;
- anyone in the home has worsening allergy or breathing symptoms.

A heating engineer can check your system and combustion appliances, and a doctor can advise on allergies.
